25:T1c18,<section id="part-0" class="reader-section"><h3>Lesson 1 — What Is Stress?</h3>
              <h4>Defining Stress</h4>
              <p>Stress is the body's response to any demand placed on it. It is not inherently bad — some stress sharpens focus, fuels motivation, and helps you perform. The key is whether the stress is short-term and manageable, or chronic and overwhelming.</p>
              <div class="reading-callout">
                <div class="callout-label">Key Idea</div>
                <div>A stressor is anything that causes stress. Stressors can be physical (illness, lack of sleep), emotional (breakups, loss), or environmental (noise, crowding). Your perception of a stressor matters as much as the stressor itself.</div>
              </div>
              <h4>Types of Stress</h4>
              <div class="reading-grid compact">
                <div class="reading-tile"><h3>Eustress</h3><p>Positive stress that motivates you — like excitement before a big game or a first date.</p></div>
                <div class="reading-tile"><h3>Distress</h3><p>Negative stress that feels overwhelming or uncontrollable — chronic worry, burnout, anxiety.</p></div>
                <div class="reading-tile"><h3>Acute stress</h3><p>Short-term, intense stress that passes once the event ends.</p></div>
                <div class="reading-tile"><h3>Chronic stress</h3><p>Long-term, persistent stress that takes a serious toll on mental and physical health.</p></div>
              </div>
            </section>
            <section id="part-1" class="reader-section"><h3>Lesson 2 — The Body's Response to Stress</h3>
              <p>When you encounter a stressor, your body automatically activates the fight-or-flight response — a survival mechanism hardwired into your nervous system. Your brain releases adrenaline and cortisol, your heart rate increases, muscles tense, and you become hyperaware of your surroundings.</p>
              <h4>The General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS)</h4>
              <ul class="reader-list">
                <li><strong>Alarm Stage:</strong> The body detects the stressor and triggers the fight-or-flight response.</li>
                <li><strong>Resistance Stage:</strong> The body attempts to cope and adapt. Resources are being used up.</li>
                <li><strong>Exhaustion Stage:</strong> If stress continues too long without relief, the body runs out of reserves and becomes vulnerable to illness.</li>
              </ul>
              <div class="reading-check">
                <strong>Physical Toll</strong>
                <span>Chronic stress is linked to heart disease, weakened immunity, digestive problems, insomnia, and depression. Managing stress is literally life-saving.</span>
              </div>
            </section>
            <section id="part-2" class="reader-section"><h3>Lesson 3 — Stress Management Techniques</h3>
              <p>You cannot eliminate all stressors, but you can control how you respond to them. Effective stress management is a skill — and like any skill, it improves with practice.</p>
              <div class="reading-grid">
                <div class="reading-tile"><h3>Physical Methods</h3><p>Exercise, progressive muscle relaxation, deep breathing, yoga, adequate sleep.</p></div>
                <div class="reading-tile"><h3>Mental Methods</h3><p>Reframing (changing how you interpret a stressor), mindfulness, journaling, positive self-talk.</p></div>
                <div class="reading-tile"><h3>Social Methods</h3><p>Talking with a trusted person, laughing with friends, joining a group or club.</p></div>
                <div class="reading-tile"><h3>Time Management</h3><p>Prioritizing tasks, breaking large projects into steps, saying no when your plate is full.</p></div>
              </div>
              <h4>The 4 A's of Stress Management</h4>
              <ul class="reader-list">
                <li><strong>Avoid:</strong> Remove the stressor if possible.</li>
                <li><strong>Alter:</strong> Change the situation by communicating differently.</li>
                <li><strong>Accept:</strong> Accept what you cannot change and adjust your response.</li>
                <li><strong>Adapt:</strong> Reframe the stressor into a growth opportunity.</li>
              </ul>
            </section>
            <section id="part-3" class="reader-section"><h3>Lesson 4 — Resilience and Post-Traumatic Growth</h3>
              <p>Resilience is not about avoiding stress — it is about developing the capacity to move through it. People with high resilience do not experience less hardship; they recover more quickly and often emerge stronger.</p>
              <h4>Building Resilience</h4>
              <ul class="reader-checklist">
                <li>Maintain strong social connections — isolation makes stress worse</li>
                <li>Keep a routine — predictability reduces anxiety</li>
                <li>Find meaning — people who see purpose in hardship recover faster</li>
                <li>Take care of basics — sleep, nutrition, and exercise form the foundation</li>
                <li>Practice optimism — not denial, but the belief that things can improve</li>
              </ul>
              <p>Post-traumatic growth is real: many people report that their most difficult experiences ultimately helped them develop greater strength, deeper relationships, and a clearer sense of what matters in life.</p>
            </section>
            <section id="part-4" class="reader-section vocab-section"><h3>Chapter Vocabulary</h3>
              <div class="vocab-table">
                <div><strong>Stress</strong><span>The body's response to real or imagined demands or threats.</span></div>
                <div><strong>Stressor</strong><span>Any situation or event that causes stress.</span></div>
                <div><strong>Eustress</strong><span>Positive stress that motivates and energizes a person.</span></div>
                <div><strong>Distress</strong><span>Negative stress that feels uncontrollable and depletes the body and mind.</span></div>
                <div><strong>Fight-or-flight response</strong><span>The body's automatic physical reaction to a perceived threat, preparing you to confront or flee.</span></div>
                <div><strong>Adrenaline</strong><span>A hormone released during the stress response that increases heart rate and energy.</span></div>
                <div><strong>Cortisol</strong><span>The primary stress hormone; elevated levels over time harm immunity, sleep, and mood.</span></div>
                <div><strong>General Adaptation Syndrome</strong><span>The three-stage process (alarm, resistance, exhaustion) the body goes through when exposed to prolonged stress.</span></div>
                <div><strong>Resilience</strong><span>The ability to recover from stress, trauma, and adversity.</span></div>
                <div><strong>Mindfulness</strong><span>The practice of focusing attention on the present moment without judgment.</span></div>
