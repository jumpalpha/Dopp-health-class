25:T1ec0,<section id="part-0" class="reader-section"><h3>Lesson 1 — Mental Disorders</h3>
              <h4>What Is a Mental Disorder?</h4>
              <p>A mental disorder is an illness that affects a person's thinking, feelings, or behavior to the point where it interferes with normal functioning. Mental disorders are medical conditions — they have biological, psychological, and social causes, just like physical diseases.</p>
              <div class="reading-callout">
                <div class="callout-label">Key Idea</div>
                <div>Mental disorders are not character flaws or signs of weakness. They are diagnosable, treatable medical conditions. About 50% of all mental disorders begin by age 14.</div>
              </div>
              <h4>Common Mental Disorders in Teens</h4>
              <div class="reading-grid compact">
                <div class="reading-tile"><h3>Anxiety Disorders</h3><p>Excessive, persistent fear or worry that interferes with daily life. Includes generalized anxiety, panic disorder, social anxiety, and phobias.</p></div>
                <div class="reading-tile"><h3>Depression</h3><p>Persistent sadness, loss of interest, low energy, and hopelessness lasting more than two weeks.</p></div>
                <div class="reading-tile"><h3>ADHD</h3><p>Difficulty with attention, impulse control, and sometimes hyperactivity. Very common and highly manageable with the right support.</p></div>
                <div class="reading-tile"><h3>Eating Disorders</h3><p>Unhealthy relationships with food and body image, including anorexia, bulimia, and binge eating disorder.</p></div>
                <div class="reading-tile"><h3>PTSD</h3><p>Post-traumatic stress disorder develops after experiencing or witnessing a traumatic event.</p></div>
                <div class="reading-tile"><h3>OCD</h3><p>Obsessive-compulsive disorder involves recurring, unwanted thoughts and repetitive behaviors.</p></div>
              </div>
            </section>
            <section id="part-1" class="reader-section"><h3>Lesson 2 — Depression and Anxiety</h3>
              <p>Depression and anxiety are the two most common mental health issues among teenagers. Understanding them — what they look like, what causes them, and how they are treated — is essential health literacy.</p>
              <h4>Warning Signs of Depression</h4>
              <ul class="reader-list">
                <li>Persistent sadness, emptiness, or hopelessness</li>
                <li>Loss of interest in activities once enjoyed</li>
                <li>Changes in sleep (too much or too little)</li>
                <li>Changes in appetite or weight</li>
                <li>Difficulty concentrating, making decisions</li>
                <li>Fatigue or loss of energy nearly every day</li>
                <li>Feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt</li>
              </ul>
              <h4>Warning Signs of Anxiety</h4>
              <ul class="reader-list">
                <li>Constant worry that is hard to control</li>
                <li>Physical symptoms: racing heart, sweating, trembling</li>
                <li>Avoiding situations that trigger fear</li>
                <li>Trouble sleeping due to worry</li>
                <li>Irritability and difficulty concentrating</li>
              </ul>
            </section>
            <section id="part-2" class="reader-section"><h3>Lesson 3 — Suicide Prevention</h3>
              <p>Suicide is the second leading cause of death for people aged 10–34 in the United States. It is preventable. Most people who consider suicide do not want to die — they want the pain to stop. Knowing the warning signs and how to respond saves lives.</p>
              <h4>Warning Signs</h4>
              <ul class="reader-list">
                <li>Talking about wanting to die or having no reason to live</li>
                <li>Giving away prized possessions</li>
                <li>Saying goodbye to people as if they will not see them again</li>
                <li>Sudden calmness after a period of deep depression</li>
                <li>Researching methods of self-harm</li>
              </ul>
              <div class="reading-check">
                <strong>What To Do</strong>
                <span>Ask directly, listen without judgment, and get help immediately. Never promise to keep a suicide crisis secret. Call or text 988 (Suicide &amp; Crisis Lifeline) or go to the nearest emergency room.</span>
              </div>
            </section>
            <section id="part-3" class="reader-section"><h3>Lesson 4 — Getting Help</h3>
              <p>Treatment works. Most people with mental health conditions improve significantly with the right support. The most important step is reaching out.</p>
              <h4>Types of Professional Help</h4>
              <div class="reading-grid">
                <div class="reading-tile"><h3>Therapist / Counselor</h3><p>Provides talk therapy to help understand and change thought and behavior patterns. No medication involved.</p></div>
                <div class="reading-tile"><h3>Psychiatrist</h3><p>A medical doctor who can diagnose mental disorders and prescribe medication.</p></div>
                <div class="reading-tile"><h3>School Counselor</h3><p>First point of contact for many teens — can provide support and connect students to community resources.</p></div>
                <div class="reading-tile"><h3>Crisis Lines</h3><p>988 (call/text) for suicide and mental health crises. Available 24/7, free, and confidential.</p></div>
              </div>
              <ul class="reader-checklist">
                <li>Asking for help is the bravest thing you can do</li>
                <li>Mental illness is not a personal failure</li>
                <li>Recovery is possible — most people get better with treatment</li>
                <li>You do not have to wait until you are in crisis to seek support</li>
              </ul>
            </section>
