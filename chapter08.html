Lesson 1 — Safe and Healthy Friendships

Types of Friendships

Your peers, people your age with similar interests, have a powerful influence on your health. Strong friendships share similar values, honest communication, mutual respect, concern for each other's safety, and sharing of joys and disappointments.

Casual

Built on shared interests without deep emotional bonds. You enjoy each other but don't always confide.

Close

Deep emotional ties. You can share thoughts, feelings and struggles without judgment.

Platonic

A friendship with someone of a different gender, with real affection but no romance.

Online Friendships — Stay Safe

  • Never share your full name, address, phone number or school.
  • Don't send photos to people you haven't met in person.
  • Never meet an online contact alone. Always tell a trusted adult first.
  • If someone online makes you uncomfortable, tell an adult right away.
  • Be careful in gaming chats, social media DMs and apps that connect you with strangers.
Key Idea
A clique can feel like belonging, but it often comes with pressure to conform and to leave others out. Healthy friendships expand your world. They don't shrink it.

Lesson 2 — Peer Pressure and Refusal Skills

What Is Peer Pressure?

Peer pressure is the influence people your age have on your actions and decisions. It can be direct or subtle. Not all of it is bad: positive peer pressure can push you to try new activities, volunteer, work harder and avoid risky behavior.

Negative pressure can show up as harassment (repeatedly targeting someone) or manipulation (indirect, dishonest control through guilt trips, threats, flattery or bribes).

Three Refusal Steps

  • 1. State your position. Say no clearly and firmly, with an honest reason: "It goes against my values." Use confident body language.
  • 2. Suggest alternatives. "No, let's go to the movies instead." You stay connected without giving up your values.
  • 3. Stand your ground. If the pressure keeps coming, make it clear you won't budge. Walking away is always an option.
Try This
Practice your refusals out loud before you're in a high-pressure moment. It's much easier to hold your ground when you already know what you'll say.

Lesson 3 — Practicing Abstinence

Dating and Setting Limits

Dating can be a positive experience when both people respect each other. The most important limit you can set is abstinence: a deliberate decision to avoid high-risk behaviors, including sexual activity and the use of alcohol, tobacco and other drugs. Self-control means letting your values, not the emotions of the moment, guide your choices.

Why Abstinence Matters

Unplanned pregnancy

Teen birth rates are at a record low, but an unplanned pregnancy still changes the course of thousands of young lives every year.

STIs

People ages 15–24 are about 13% of the population but have nearly half of new STIs. Abstinence is the only 100% effective protection.

Emotional risks

Hurt, guilt, loss of self-respect and regret are common when teens aren't ready for a sexual relationship.

Media pressure

Shows and social media glamorize sex without showing consequences. That's entertainment, not reality.

Handling Pressure Lines

  • "Everybody does it." → "No. Not everybody is doing it."
  • "No one will know." → "I'll know, and I'm the one who matters."
  • "If you loved me, you'd do it." → "If you loved me, you'd respect my decision."
Key Idea
It's never too late. Anyone can choose abstinence going forward, and it's a positive choice that protects your health and future.

Chapter Vocabulary

PeersPeople your age who share similar interests.
Platonic friendshipA friendship with someone of a different gender involving genuine affection, but without romantic involvement.
CliqueA small circle of friends who exclude people viewed as outsiders.
Peer pressureThe influence that people your age may have on your actions and behaviors.
HarassmentPersistently annoying or targeting others through hurtful behaviors like name-calling, teasing, or bullying.
ManipulationAn indirect, dishonest way to control or influence other people.
Refusal skillsCommunication strategies that help you say no when urged to take part in unsafe or unhealthy behaviors.
PrioritiesThe goals, tasks, values, and activities you judge to be most important.
IntimacyA closeness between two people that develops over time through genuine connection and trust.
InfatuationExaggerated feelings of intense passion that can feel overwhelming but are often short-lived.
AbstinenceA deliberate decision to avoid high-risk behaviors, including sexual activity and the use of alcohol, tobacco, and other drugs.
Self-controlThe ability to use responsibility and values to override emotions in the moment.
Sexually transmitted infections (STIs)Infectious diseases spread from person to person through sexual contact.
