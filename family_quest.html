Roots & Branches

Map Your Family. Find Your Story. You Can't Pick Your Family!

Chapters 6–9 · 4 class days · 100 points · Every family is messy, and that's the point.

1The Big Idea: You Can't Pick Your Family

Every family looks a little messy up close. Divorce, remarriage, step and half siblings, people who passed away, people you don't talk to, and people you know nothing about are all normal parts of a family map.

In this quest you will map your family, research where it came from, collect one family story, and reflect on what you can and can't control.

🎯 You will…

  • Draw your family relationships
  • Research your family history
  • Interview a relative for a story
  • Pick one relationship to improve

🧭 Day 1 warm-up

  1. One thing about your family you'd never change?
  2. One thing you didn't choose and can't control?
  3. Why does every family look messy up close?

3Part 1: Draw My Family Relationships · Required · 40 pts

Draw your family as a map of people and the lines that connect them. Start with you, go up to your grandparents on both sides, and out to aunts, uncles, cousins, siblings, nieces, and nephews. Stick figures are fine. Clear beats pretty.

Your drawing must include:

  • You, labeled "ME"
  • Parents & siblings
  • Grandparents (both sides)
  • Aunts & uncles + spouses
  • Cousins
  • Nieces & nephews (if any)
  • Lines connecting everyone
  • A legend / key
  • Names or titles (your choice)

Legend symbols you need (use these or make your own, as long as your key explains them):

🌀 Messy is the point. If your family has divorces, remarriages, unknowns, or people you don't talk to, put them on the map. You never have to explain why. An honest map earns full credit.

5Part 2: Family History Research · 20 pts

Everyone completes a 4-generation chart (you → parents → grandparents → great-grandparents) and one ancestor spotlight. Pick Path A or Path B. Both earn full credit.

🌳 Path A: FamilySearch (encouraged)

  1. Make a free account at FamilySearch.org. Under 18? You may need a parent's help.
  2. Add yourself, parents, grandparents, and great-grandparents to the Family Tree. Living people stay private, so you enter them.
  3. Once you reach ancestors who have passed away, look for records about them.
  4. Screenshot your tree or fan chart.

📄 Path B: Paper (no account)

  1. Print the 4-generation chart below.
  2. Fill it in with a parent, grandparent, or other relative.
  3. Use what your family already has: scrapbooks, obituaries, old photos with names on the back.

Ancestor spotlight (both paths): pick one grandparent or older ancestor and write one paragraph:

  • Name, about when they were born, and where they lived
  • Their work, and one hardship or big event they lived through
  • One fact you learned and one question you still have

7Part 3: Family Story Interview · 20 pts

Interview one relative for 15–20 minutes, ideally a grandparent or the oldest relative you can reach. In person, phone, or video all work. Ask at least 6 of these:

  1. Where did you grow up, and what was a normal day like?
  2. What did your parents do for work?
  3. What family tradition started with you or someone before you?
  4. What was the hardest thing our family went through, and how did you get through it?
  5. Who were you closest to growing up, and why?
  6. Was there a hard relationship in the family? What did you learn from it?
  7. What's a funny story about someone in our family?
  8. What do you wish more people in our family knew?
  9. What's one piece of advice about family you'd give me?
  10. Is there anything you'd go back and do differently with family?
✍ Write-up (1 page): retell the best story in your own words. End with 2–3 sentences on what it taught you about your family.

8Part 4: What I Can and Can't Control · 20 pts

Look at your finished map. Answer in full sentences (about 1 page).

⭕ Inside my circle (I can control)

List 3: my words, my effort, my attitude, my boundaries, whether I forgive.

🌫 Outside my circle (I can't)

List 3: other people's choices, divorces, moves, who is in my family.
  1. The relationship I want to improve: pick one person on your map. What's it like now? What would "better" look like?
  2. My next step: one specific thing you'll do in the next two weeks (a text, a visit, a thank-you, an apology, asking about their life).
  3. A choice I didn't make: describe a decision someone in your family made that affects you. Leave out names or details if you want. What would accepting it look like? Accepting isn't the same as agreeing.
  4. You can't pick your family: after making this map, what does that mean to you now?
  5. Grateful for: one person or pattern on your map you're thankful for, and why.

10If This Is Hard for You

Some families carry hard stuff. The drawing is still required, and here's how it works for you:

  • Adopted or in foster care? Draw your birth family, your adoptive or foster family, or both. Any of them counts.
  • Don't know someone? A "?" box gets full credit.
  • No explaining. You never have to say why a divorce, a death, or a break in contact happened.
  • Sharing is your choice. On Day 4 you decide whether to show anything to classmates.
  • Names are optional. Titles like "Aunt" or "Grandpa" are fine.
  • Need to talk? Mr. Dopp and the school counselors are here for you.

You can't pick your family.
You can pick how you show up for them.
